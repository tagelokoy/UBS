01The problem

Batteries die with the wrong thing.

Most rechargeable things glue a battery pack inside. Sometimes the cells outlast the device, sometimes the device outlasts the cells. Either way, both go in the bin together. Loose cells exist, but buying them is a specialist hobby.

02Already half-done

Tool brands proved it. Then they kept it to themselves.

Ryobi ONE+, Makita LXT, Milwaukee M18 and DeWalt each run a whole catalogue of tools from one battery. Alliances like Bosch's Power for All and Metabo's CAS go further and share one battery across several brands. People love these systems, and they prove the idea works. But every one of them stops at the edge of a brand or a club. UBS takes the same idea and makes it public.

The law is moving the same way. From February 2027, EU rules require batteries in most portable appliances to be removable and replaceable by the user. UBS is one answer to how. Heavy tools will keep their packs for a while: a 1 kW grinder draws more than small per-slot converters handle well. UBS starts where the power is modest.

UBSThe standard · Draft 0.3

Five rules. The rest is up to the device.

UBS is deliberately small. It defines the cell, how the cell tells you what it is, and how it keeps itself safe. Devices are free to be whatever they want around it.

§1 · Formats

Three sizes, named the way you already name batteries.

UBS borrows the letter logic of AA and AAA: more letters, smaller cell. Each size is built on a cell format that is already made by the billion. The outer size is fixed and includes the protection circuit, so a BB is always 69.0 mm long.

BBB has the same diameter as an AA but runs at 3.2–3.6 V instead of 1.5 V. It is 2.5 mm longer on purpose, so it won't close into most AA compartments. Whether that is enough is an open question for draft 0.4.

§2 · Chemistry

Color says the chemistry. Bands say it again.

About one in twelve men has some form of color blindness, and green against orange is one of the hardest pairs. So every UBS cell also carries dark bands near its negative end: one for Li-ion, two for LiFePO₄, three for sodium-ion. You can read them by touch, too.

Device rule: a UBS device must run on every approved chemistry in its size. Today that means accepting 2.0 to 4.2 V per cell. Chargers pick the charge profile from the passport, never from a guess.

§3 · Protection  /  §4 · Passport

Every cell protects itself and remembers its own life.

Unprotected cells are fine for people who know battery safety. A standard for everyone can't assume that. Every UBS cell carries a protection circuit in its cap, so a cell in a pocket full of keys, a cheap device or a broken charger stays safe.

The same cap holds a fuel gauge and an NFC chip: the passport. Hold a cell to your phone and it shows its charge, health and history, even while it's running inside a device with a clear housing. It needs no extra contacts, so any device works with it without knowing it's there. The data format is open, so any app can read it. No account, no cloud.

§5 · Rating code

One code for energy and power.

A cell has two numbers that matter: how much energy it holds and how hard it can be pushed. Inside one size, they trade off against each other. UBS writes both into a two-digit code, the same way IP67 packs dust and water protection into one mark. A higher first digit runs longer. A higher second digit pushes harder.

Devices print what they need the same way: UBS BB · P5 means any BB cell with a power class of 5 or higher runs the device at full power. A weaker cell still works; the device throttles.

UBS§6 · Bays · optional part of the spec

Use whatever cells you have.

A UBS bay is a set of universal slots, each with its own small converter. The converters feed one shared power line, so the device never sees the cells directly. That means a bay can take two B cells today and three BB cells tomorrow, mixed sizes, mixed chemistries, mixed health. Try it.

Cost

About 8% of the energy

Per-slot converters lose some power as heat. In return you never need matched cells, and a tired cell can't drag down a fresh one.

Share

Each cell pulls its weight

The bay draws from each slot in proportion to its remaining energy, so all cells run out together and none is pushed past its rating.

Choice

Weight or runtime, your call

One B cell for a light hike. Three for a week at the cabin. The device is the same.

Any way in PROPOSAL

Orientation stops mattering

Each slot already has electronics, so it can flip polarity too. Slide a cell in either way round. Cells keep their + end, so they still work in simple holders.

UBS§7 · Return credit · a condition of using the UBS mark

Bring it back. Get €3.

Take any worn-out UBS cell, from any maker, to any shop that sells UBS cells. You get €3 off new UBS cells for every cell you hand in. There's no deposit on the receipt and nothing to keep track of. The cell itself proves it's genuine.

  1. Bring any UBS cell

    Dead, weak, or flagged for retirement by your charger or the Pool app.

  2. The shop taps it

    NFC checks the cell's signed ID and marks it returned. A cell can only be credited once, and fakes don't pass.

  3. Get €3 off new cells

    Spend the credit on any UBS cell from any maker, in that shop or any other.

  4. Sorted before it ships

    The passport states the chemistry, so Li-ion and LiFePO₄ go to the right recycling process without anyone guessing.

Why it matters

Cells in the bin start fires

Lithium cells thrown in household waste get crushed in bin lorries and sorting plants, and some catch fire. Every cell that comes back is one that doesn't.

What comes back

Most of the metal

Once collected, most of a cell can be recovered. EU rules require recyclers to recover 90% of the cobalt, nickel and copper and 50% of the lithium by the end of 2027, rising to 95% and 80% by 2031.

Who pays

Makers, through a shared pool

Every maker pays €3 into a shared pool for each UBS cell it sells. Shops claim back the credits they hand out, so a shop that takes back more than it sells isn't out of pocket.

Open questions · for draft 0.4

What we haven't solved yet.

An open standard should say where it's unfinished. These are the questions on the table. Comments are open to anyone.

§1

Keeping BBB out of AA devices

A longer body stops most compartments from closing, but not spring-loaded ones. A keyed shoulder on the positive end is the leading proposal.

§4

NFC on a metal can

A steel can blocks NFC unless a thin ferrite layer sits under the antenna. That works, but metal device housings still block live reading.

§4

Reading a whole drawer

Phones read one NFC tag at a time. Chargers can read every slot at once; a phone taps cell after cell. Is that fast enough?

§6

Any-way-in slots

Polarity-free slots are cheap in a bay but add loss and cost in simple devices. Should they be required, optional, or left out?

§7

Credit or cash

A credit only helps people who buy new cells. Should the last cell of a household pay out in cash instead?

§5

Who tests the ratings

A public method is a start. Without independent spot checks, "BB·74" can become the new "9900 mAh".

§2

Charging inside devices

In-device charging is allowed today if the device reads the passport. Should a device that can't read it be allowed to charge at all?

§6

Bay efficiency

Per-slot converters cost around 8%. A bypass mode when all cells match could win most of that back.

§2

Sodium-ion approval

Sodium-ion cells can sit at 0 V safely, which breaks the over-discharge rule. The spec needs a chemistry-aware version.
